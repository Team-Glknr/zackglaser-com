# Zack Glaser blog and article writing style guide

## Purpose

This guide defines Zack Glaser's voice and editorial preferences for blog posts, articles, essays, and thought-leadership content. Use it when outlining, drafting, revising, or evaluating work intended to sound like Zack.

The goal is not merely to reproduce surface-level quirks. The writing should reflect how Zack develops an idea: identify something useful or underexamined, test the conventional framing, explain the practical consequences, and give the reader a clearer way to think or act.

## Core voice

Zack's writing is:

- Smart without showing off
- Direct without being blunt
- Conversational without becoming casual or sloppy
- Confident without pretending to have every answer
- Witty without performing for laughs
- Practical without reducing every idea to a list of tips
- Skeptical of easy narratives, empty conventions, and performative expertise
- Grounded in experience, professional judgment, and the realities of doing the work

The reader should feel that a knowledgeable colleague is thinking through an important issue with them — not lecturing them, coaching them, or marketing at them.

## The intellectual posture

Zack's strongest pieces usually do more than deliver information. They make a distinction that helps the reader see a familiar subject differently.

Common moves include:

1. Start with a recognizable situation, claim, tension, or frustration.
2. Identify what the usual framing misses.
3. Separate ideas that people tend to collapse together.
4. Examine what changes when the distinction is taken seriously.
5. Translate the insight into practical consequences.
6. End with a useful conclusion, challenge, or next step — not a generic inspirational flourish.

The writing may question conventional wisdom, but it should not be contrarian merely for effect. Criticism should lead somewhere useful.

## Tone

Aim for a tone that is informed, approachable, and lightly irreverent.

### Do

- Write with a natural rhythm that resembles intelligent speech.
- Use plain language, even when discussing sophisticated ideas.
- Make confident claims when the evidence or experience supports them.
- Acknowledge uncertainty when it matters.
- Use occasional dry humor, understated wit, or a clever aside.
- Use parenthetical asides when they add context, precision, or a quiet joke.
- Vary sentence length to create rhythm and emphasis.
- Prefer specific observations over sweeping declarations.
- Keep the reader oriented around why the idea matters.

### Do not

- Use generic marketing language.
- Sound peppy, breathless, inspirational, or overly enthusiastic.
- Write like a motivational coach.
- Use jargon to manufacture authority.
- Drift into legalese or academic formality.
- Overuse contractions.
- Overuse rhetorical questions.
- Insert humor that distracts from the argument.
- Flatten nuanced ideas into slogans.
- Repeat the same point in several slightly different ways.

## Humor and asides

Humor should strengthen clarity or connection. It often works best as a brief aside, a restrained observation, or a precise description of something mildly absurd.

Good humor:

- Reveals the weakness in a familiar assumption.
- Gives the reader a moment of recognition.
- Makes a dense idea easier to absorb.
- Sounds spontaneous but remains economical.

Avoid jokes that make the piece feel flippant, snarky, or less trustworthy. One sharp aside is usually better than a paragraph built around the joke.

## Audience relationship

Treat the reader as capable and busy. Do not overexplain the obvious, but do not assume specialized knowledge unless the audience clearly has it.

Zack's primary audiences often include lawyers, law-firm leaders, legal professionals, consultants, and people thinking seriously about technology, AI, management, and professional work. The writing should respect their expertise while still challenging habits or assumptions that deserve scrutiny.

Avoid positioning Zack above the reader. Prefer the posture of an experienced peer who has noticed something worth examining.

## Structure

Use clear sections when they help the reader follow the argument. The structure should feel driven by the idea, not imposed by a content template.

A typical article may use this shape:

### 1. Opening

Begin with the actual issue. Good openings include:

- A concrete observation
- A tension or contradiction
- A short anecdote that earns its place
- A familiar claim that needs closer examination
- A direct statement of the problem

Avoid broad scene-setting, dictionary definitions, and throat-clearing such as "in today's rapidly changing world."

### 2. Reframing

Explain why the obvious interpretation is incomplete. Introduce the distinction, overlooked assumption, or better question at the center of the piece.

### 3. Development

Build the argument through examples, consequences, counterpoints, or practical experience. Each section should advance the idea rather than restate it.

### 4. Application

Show what the reader should notice, reconsider, decide, or do differently. Advice should follow from the argument rather than appearing as a disconnected list of best practices.

### 5. Ending

End crisply. A strong ending may:

- Return to the opening idea with a sharper interpretation.
- State the practical implication.
- Offer a meaningful challenge or next step.
- Leave the reader with a useful distinction.

Avoid generic summaries, forced optimism, and calls to "embrace the future."

## Headlines and subheadings

Headlines should promise a clear idea rather than manufacture curiosity.

Prefer headlines that are:

- Specific
- Intellectually interesting
- Useful without sounding transactional
- Conversational but not cute
- Faithful to the argument of the piece

Avoid clickbait, exaggerated stakes, and formulaic constructions unless the format genuinely serves the idea.

Follow Chicago-style headline capitalization, with one house exception: capitalize "To" in "How To" headlines.

Subheadings should help the reader navigate the argument. They should describe the point of the section, not merely label its topic.

## Language choices

Prefer simple, precise words.

| Avoid | Use |
| --- | --- |
| utilize | use |
| upon | on |
| judgement | judgment |
| leads | potential clients |
| customer | client |
| whom | who |

Use "they" and "their" as the default pronouns when gender is unknown or irrelevant.

Avoid:

- Corporate filler such as "leverage," "synergy," "game-changing," and "best-in-class"
- AI clichés such as "revolutionize," "unlock," "supercharge," and "the future is here"
- Empty transitions such as "it is important to note that"
- Inflated claims that the piece cannot support
- Unnecessary qualifiers that weaken otherwise sound sentences

## Punctuation and formatting

- Use proper em dashes — without spaces on either side.
- Use one space between sentences.
- Use ellipses with appropriate spacing.
- Use italics for emphasis and for terms being introduced or discussed as terms.
- Use quotation marks for interface labels and quoted words or phrases.
- Follow the Chicago Manual of Style for capitalization, subject to the "How To" exception above.
- Do not use emojis.
- Use lists when they genuinely improve comprehension, not as the automatic form for every argument.

## Questions

Questions can be useful, but they should not become a substitute for making the point.

Use a question when it:

- Identifies the real issue the reader should consider.
- Creates a meaningful transition.
- Invites reflection that the following discussion will reward.

Avoid strings of rhetorical questions and openings built entirely around questions. When the answer is clear, state it.

## Calls to action

Calls to action should match the purpose of the piece and feel like a natural extension of the argument.

Good calls to action may ask the reader to:

- Reconsider a familiar practice.
- Try a specific exercise or process.
- Discuss an issue with their team.
- Read, listen to, or explore a genuinely relevant resource.
- Contact the organization when the article has established a credible reason to do so.

Avoid abrupt sales pitches, manufactured urgency, and vague invitations to "learn more."

## Drafting workflow

Before drafting, establish:

1. The intended audience
2. The purpose of the piece
3. The central claim or useful distinction
4. The desired reader response
5. The target length
6. Any publication, brand, keyword, or call-to-action requirements
7. Whether Zack already has an opening, anecdote, example, or conclusion in mind

If the target length is not provided, ask for it. Ask only the questions that will materially affect the draft.

When useful, begin with a proposed structure so the argument can be tested before the prose is polished.

## Revision priorities

Revise in this order:

1. **Argument:** Is there a clear idea worth reading?
2. **Structure:** Does each section advance that idea?
3. **Utility:** Will the reader understand why it matters?
4. **Voice:** Does it sound like Zack rather than a generic content writer?
5. **Clarity:** Can any sentence be shorter or more precise?
6. **Rhythm:** Does the prose vary naturally without becoming choppy?
7. **Mechanics:** Does it follow the house rules for wording, punctuation, and capitalization?

## Final voice check

Before delivering a draft, ask:

- Does the piece contain a real insight, distinction, or useful point of view?
- Does the opening reach the issue quickly?
- Does the draft sound like an informed person thinking clearly rather than a brand producing content?
- Is the confidence supported by reasoning, evidence, or experience?
- Does every section add something new?
- Is the humor restrained and useful?
- Have rhetorical questions and contractions been used sparingly?
- Has generic marketing language been removed?
- Is the conclusion earned and specific?
- Are the house-style replacements and punctuation rules followed?

Develop a clear insight or distinction, test conventional assumptions where useful, and connect the argument to real consequences. Use plain language and a natural spoken rhythm, with occasional concise parenthetical asides or dry humor. Be confident but not heavy-handed, conversational but not casual, and useful without defaulting to a generic listicle.
