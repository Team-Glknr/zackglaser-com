AI-assisted writing has an obvious failure mode: it flattens. Feed a model a topic and ask for an essay, and you get competent, smooth, forgettable prose — the same register no matter the subject, all the edges sanded off. That complaint is fair, and it isn't going away just because the tools get better at sounding human.

But there's a second failure mode that predates AI entirely, and almost nobody talks about it: most writing has always had to pick one altitude and stay there. An essay is a curated take — it has to leave things out to be readable at all. That's not a flaw, it's the job. The problem is that "curated take" has historically been the *only* altitude on offer, not because anyone chose it on purpose, but because the alternative — actually publishing the raw material underneath the take, organized well enough that a reader would ever want to go there — cost more time than almost anyone had to spend. So most work stayed single-altitude by default, not by decision.

This week I published two things that sit at different altitudes of the same underlying work. ["Stop hiding the seams"](/field-notes/stop-hiding-the-seams) is the curated take — an argument, compressed, with everything that doesn't serve the point left out on purpose. The [build log behind this site](/workshop/building-this-site) is the other altitude entirely: twenty-five dated entries, sorted into chapters, each one a real account of a decision as it happened, dead ends included, nothing smoothed over after the fact. Same underlying work. Two different doors into it, built for two different readers.

Here's the actual claim, and it's a narrower one than "AI makes things more honest" — that second door existing at all is new math, not a stylistic choice I'd have made regardless. Sorting twenty-five entries into seven chapters, each with its own real recap, keeping the whole thing consistent and actually navigable instead of a folder of markdown nobody would open — that's exactly the kind of structural labor that used to lose to "just ship the essay and move on." AI assistance is what makes that labor affordable enough to actually do. Not because it wrote the entries — the decisions, the dead ends, the things I got wrong are mine — but because organizing and maintaining a second, deeper layer of the same material stopped being a cost nobody could justify.

That's the part I think is genuinely new, not just AI doing an old job faster: a reader gets to pick their own depth instead of one piece of writing being asked to do every job at once. Someone who wants the argument gets the argument, at essay length, without it bloating to cover everything. Someone who wants the texture underneath — how the decision actually got made, what got tried and abandoned first — gets a real, structured place to go looking, instead of a summary standing in for something they'd rather see for themselves.

None of this works if the self-serve layer is unreadable. A raw commit log with no organization is just as useless as an essay with no substance — maybe more so, since nobody climbs into a folder of unsorted files far enough to find out what's actually in there. The chapters exist for exactly that reason: a whole project's worth of entries is too much to hand someone raw, but seven readable groups, each with its own short recap and its own stats, means the depth is actually reachable instead of theoretical. Structure is what turns "more information" into "more entry points" instead of just more noise.

So that's the working theory this newsletter is going to test, issue by issue, not just this one: whether a subject needs a single curated take, or whether it's worth building the self-serve layer underneath it too — an abstract and the raw material, published side by side, each doing a job the other one can't. That's a real judgment call every time, not a rule I'm claiming always applies. This week, for this site, I think it was worth building both.

— Zack
